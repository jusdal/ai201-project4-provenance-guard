Methods of Data Gathering in Ethnographic Research

Gathering data is a very important aspect of doing ethnographic research and there are a number of data gathering methods that can be used by anthropologists. Writing is one very important tool for gathering and transmitting data in ethnographic research as an anthropologist is not responsible for simply observing social interactions; they must also be recorded and translated to others through writing. Emerson, Fretz, and Shaw (2001) identify two main types of writing that anthropologists use in ethnographic fieldwork: polished ethnographies and fieldnotes. Polished ethnographic writing, which was the main focus of debate in ethnographic writing until the 1990s, refers to the finalized reports that are intended for publication. Polished ethnographic writing has a specific purpose and is written after the anthropologist has left the field.
Emerson, Fretz, and Shaw contrast polished ethnographic writing with ethnographic fieldnotes to highlight several of their elements and show how critical they are to the process of ethnography. First, because fieldnotes are written in (or close to) the field, they are written almost simultaneously with the social interactions they are describing providing little time for deep analysis. Contrary to polished writing, they are not intended for an audience and they have no apparent purpose when read alone. Second, fieldnotes are written accounts of social worlds and are therefore representations. The anthropologist chooses what to record and this process is inherently selective as there is no way to capture everything in a social interaction through writing. Third, fieldnotes are written on a day-to-day basis and have no apparent underlying principle or connection. Observations recorded via fieldnotes build up over time into a corpus and connections can be drawn later through more analytical polished writing. Emerson, Fretz, and Shaw see fieldnotes as an essential step in ethnographic fieldwork stating that they “provide the primal, even foundational moments of ethnographic representation: for most ethnographic monographs rely upon, incorporate and may even be built from these initial fieldnotes” (352). They also point out what can be included in fieldnotes. They need not only focus on descriptive  external elements–other people, places, things, dialogues, nonverbal communication–but also the internal elements–their own emotions, actions, questions, reflections, and analysis.
Another important data gathering method utilized by anthropologists in fieldwork is structured observation. Structured observation differs from unstructured observation in that it is not simply observing some social group or phenomena and recording whatever one thinks is relevant with no planning beforehand. Like unstructured observation, it is conducted in a natural setting but, unlike unstructured observation, it is characterized by first developing a standardized protocol which is a set of formal guidelines for conducting research (Price 92). This allows the data collected to be reasonably objective and easily comparable and is great for data collection at multiple sites. This data collection method is especially important when dealing with sensitive topics in which people might provide inaccurate or misleading information through interviews. One case in which this method would be useful is researching the frequency of ID verification during the sale of alcohol or cigarettes in stores. Store employees may be reluctant to tell the truth and may overestimate the number of times they check IDs while selling alcohol or cigarettes. In order to conduct a structured observation in this scenario, one should first decide what specific questions to answer and decide what variables to measure and how to measure them and use these to develop the standardized protocol. In my example, I would develop a standardized protocol with several variables including how many times an ID is checked, approximate age of customer, other items included in the purchase, etc. I would then use this protocol to do the observation being careful of the problems faced in this method like information overload, cross-site inconsistencies, and missing information.
