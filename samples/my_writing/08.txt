3.2: ACTIVE AUDIENCE

Ien Ang (1982) provides a textual analysis of letters written by people explaining their perspective on the extremely popular American TV series 'Dallas.' She doesn't try to answer the question of why Dallas is so popular (a question that was asked by many during this time period) but rather looks at why people find it pleasurable (or not) and what that means (5). In doing this, Ang explains that the ideology of mass culture has been adopted by most people as fact. The theory, which has already been described by many of you, sets forth a binary of good culture (authentic, high brow) and bad culture (commercial, stereotypical, lowbrow). Dallas undoubtedly falls into the latter category and most lovers and haters of the show use the ideology of mass culture to explain their pleasure/displeasure derived from watching the show. The haters legitimize their hatred through this ideology (i.e. obviously this show is garbage because it's commercial and thus bad) while the fans acknowledge that it is 'bad' (thus upholding/acknowledging the ideology of mass culture) yet defend against the theory. A few people reject this ideology of mass culture, adopting her ideology of populism in which people like things based on their own taste.

I think Ang subscribes to a more nuanced view of culture than Horkheimer & Adorno's notion as she states in the introduction that it is "somewhat risky to trace the appeal of Dallas to one hidden message or meaning, for it is implausible that Moroccan, Italian, Or English viewers are all just as open to such a 'message' as Americans are - if we could even indeed lump all Americans together" (4). She looks at the way people are interacting and explaining their media consumption and likes/dislikes to develop her theory.


3.3: A MORE NUANCED AUDIENCE

William Mazzarella (2004) lays out a very complex argument in his article "Culture, Globalization, Mediation." Essentially, he complicates the historical theoretical binary developed around globalization and media (cultural imperialism ←→ active audience) and instead focuses on the processes of mediation, which he views as a general foundation of social life. He defines mediation as "processes by which a given social dispensation produces and reproduces itself in and through a particular set of media" and defines media not just as television, radio, film, etc. but also rituals. As stated before, he complicates the idea that there is, on the one hand, a hegemonizing Western-led commodification of culture leading to a passive mass audience and the duality of mass culture/authentic culture—as Horkheimer & Adorno (1947) suggested—and, on the other hand, an active audience that engages with media in their own way, "refract[ing] the would-be hegemonic discourse of national or transnational media content"—as Mankekar (1999) suggested (350). As anthropologists, he calls for us to somehow look between these two narratives to see how people are both shaping and being shaped by media through the process of mediation.

His argument is aligned with the practice theory of Bordieu, seeing culture as within each person and reenacted through interactions with other people.
