CONCLUSION

As evidenced by the review of these two perspectives, a conclusion has still not been reached on whether classificatory categories of mental illness can be applied across cultures. Edgerton called for more empirical research and many have answered this call. Unfortunately, the boundary and interaction between sociocultural factors and organic, biological factors in mental illness remains difficult to unravel and the debate persists. The introduction of the cultural appendix in the DSM-IV has shown that culture is becoming a more important component of mental health but anthropologists argue that not enough has been done to move away from the biomedical, universalist perspective. Furthermore, the inability to find biological markers and the lack of a gold standard for validating most psychiatric conditions have sustained the debate. 

Many current scholars call for a combined approach in which psychiatrists and anthropologists work together to solve mental health problems and gain knowledge about the illnesses by moving past the universalist-relativist binary. Draguns & Tanaka-Matsumi (2003) summarize: “Time is ripe for a partial shift toward a more emic orientation in conceptualization and data gathering. Eventually, we expect convergence and integration of these two approaches as etic research is informed by greater cultural sensitivity and emic studies become more objective, quantified, and rigorous” (770). Similarly, Kaiser & Kohrt (2019) have suggested that Western psychiatrists can learn from anthropologists by looking at the broader systems and structures of which they are a part, to consider what changes will make for a more reflexive, critically oriented future in psychiatry (212).
