CONCLUSION

As seen in the section on globalism, there was a longstanding notion in both history and anthropology that culture was bounded by land masses and that these locations contained more or less homogenous cultures. Furthermore, a prominent modernization theory assumed that one homogenous global culture would emerge (Taylor, 1995), subsuming the variety of existing cultures in the process. Appadurai’s more nuanced theory of fluid scapes unsettled these notions. 

The former argument was made in terms of modernity and how it would affect marriage and kinship in India. With the rise of intimacy and romantic love, many scholars proposed the growing Middle class in India would adopt these modernist views and there would be little difference between marriage in India and marriage in the West. Looking ethnographically, however, researchers saw that a unique form of marriage emerged: companionate marriage in which components of intimacy and romantic love were incorporated into the existing ideologies of kinship and the social structures of the Indian middle class (Fuller & Narasimhan, 2008). Like Appadurai suggested, the process was fluid and overlapping. 

This process seems to be mirrored in the case of Islam where literature that addresses the spread of Islam frames Islam as a ‘modern’ ideology that is taking over the cultures of existing littoral societies, imposing its global elements of prayer and ritual. Lambek’s example, like Appadurai’s, challenges this notion by showing how Islam was not forced upon them and did not overtake their way of life. Rather, they incorporated and localized Islam, through the performativity of prayer, into and perhaps strengthened their existing way of life, community, and kinship systems. Lambek points out that they did not just shape Islam. Islam also shaped them by making their social organization more egaligatarian what he refers to as the social order being Islamicized. This goes back to the fluid nature of globalization through Appadurai’s lens and, in this way, Islam can be seen as a fluid ideoscape flowing back and forth, not simply unidirectional. Deterritorialization and the idea of an imagined community is also apparent in Lambek’s example with Islam moving from its place of origin in the Arabian peninsula to Mayotte and connecting Muslims across the Indian Ocean into an imagined community constructed through Islamic texts. Localization, on the other hand, is seen by the repeated practices of localized prayer performances in Mayotte.
