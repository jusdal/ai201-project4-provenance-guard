Love Marriages in India 

Marriage in India has undergone many changes over the past century as evidenced by anthropologists. Arranged marriage has been a very important institution within India and is imperative for maintaining India’s social structure. With the rise of modernity and notions of romantic love and pure relationships, the institution of marriage in India has transformed and arranged marriages, while still the dominant type of marriage, are becoming less common. C.J. Fuller and Haripriya Narasimhan highlighted the changing aspects of arranged marriage in Middle-class India through their concept of companionate marriage, in which a family considers partner compatibility and input from their son or daughter when selecting a partner. Henrike Donner and Gonçalo Santos further illustrated the changing nature of marriage in India in their examination of the way marriage has become a negotiation between the individual desires of children and the collective interests of parents and extended families. What hasn’t been noted in detail, however, is the love marriage and its implications on kinship and social networks in Indian communities. Perveez Mody examines this notion of love marriages and its disruptive nature in The Intimate State: Love Marriage & the Law in Delhi.
Mody explains there are two types of love marriage in (Delhi) India: love-cum-arranged marriages and elopements. The love-cum-arranged marriage is a marriage in which a couple meets independently of their respective families (157). They then marry in secret but return to their families and pretend nothing happened while they try to convince their family that the person they have married is a legitimate and worthy partner. If their families agree that the partner is suitable, a second wedding is organized by their families. Usually, two separate weddings are held because a joint wedding with both families would lead to conflict. These second weddings are shrouded in deceit because the families conceal the true identity of the partner and the wedding is organized and performed as an arranged marriage. Although the marriage is legitimized through the second wedding, it is risky for the couple’s families because, if the truth is revealed that this was actually a love marriage, the entire family could be excommunicated. The love-cum-arranged marriage shows the complexity of modern marriage in India and reveals more details of the intersection of personal desire and collective interests that Donner and Santos referred to.
Elopements are marriages in which the couple meets and falls in love and gets married in secret but, instead of going back and living with their respective families, they leave their families and start a new life together. Because of the shame that elopement can bring to a family, they often file police reports and state that their child has been kidnapped or abducted. Oftentimes, the family actually knows that their child has fallen in love and most likely has run away to elope but lie to the police in order to avoid the social stigma that comes along with having a child–especially a daughter–who has eloped. If the child is found and it is determined that they have indeed eloped, the family is forced to cut ties with the child. Other times, the child stages their own abduction to save their families from this public embarrassment. 
This reveals the intricacy and the truly disruptive nature of love marriages in India. While many people in India claim that they have no problem with love marriage (as Zahra’s brother, Riaz, says 181), the reality is much more complicated. It seems that love marriage is so stigmatized that it is not a valid option for most people if they want to remain connected to their families. This leads to the disruptive nature of these kinds of marriages. Mody states that, through elopement, young people “make and break kin at will” (178). These types of love marriage also highlight the strong intersections of class and caste in India and the way that marriage is still a very important way of replicating the complex social structure there. Without the support of their family, it is difficult to know their place within society. This shows that, even with the rise of modernity and the emergence of Middle-classness in India, identity is still strongly tied to the collective there.
