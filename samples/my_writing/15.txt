Guest Lecture
Muslim Traders, Songhay Warriors, and the Arma: 
The Social Deconstruction of the Middle Niger Bend from 1549 to 1660 

	The speaker conducted research for his dissertation in history. Thus, he gives us a historical (rather than an anthropological) perspective of the Middle Niger Bend from 1549 to 1660. He began his presentation with a brief background on the geography of the region by showing a map of West Africa and pointing out important points of interest and noting that this region no longer remains like this. He says that Timbuktu is the “star of the show” which highlights its importance in the region during this time because it is an oasis in this dry region and provides opportunity for agriculture. Marrakesh is also an important place because the ruler of Morocco was there during this time. The Moroccans were in great conflict with the Ottomans during this time. The river was very important because it was a major contact zone between these groups and linked these two areas with a third region, Sudan, that was also culturally distinct. These three areas were linked by caravan routes along the river which provided the possibility to exchange not just goods but also religion and culture. The goods that were traded included gold, slaves, salt, horses, books and fabrics. 
	The speaker then told us about his first experiences in the region. He began his studies in Marrakesh by looking into archives which described people from Mali as “uncivilized” and savage because they were not Muslim and because of cannibalism. This view was echoed by his friend when he told him about his plans to go to Mali. After this archive research, he went to Mali where he began talking to people. The people there did not see Morrocans as African because of their lighter skin color. Furthermore, they viewed the invasion and attacks from the north as enslavement. 
	The presentation then turned to focus on the Songhay Empire in 1591. It started in Gao, Bentia/Kukiya and was then controlled by the Mali Empire. Under Sunni Ali, Songhay became independent and then conquered and expanded west to Timbuktu and then even further to Jenne. Three cities and areas were highlighted, each with their own specific strengths. Gao was the political center, while Timbuktu was the storehouse and Jenne was the trading hub. 
	The speaker then honed into the importance of religion in this region. Sunni Ali initially rose to power within the confines of a pre-Islamic social system which was based on honor. Social status was conferred to principles of honor. This was apparent in battle or conquering as conquering was not about territory but about controlling people. Sunni Ali was not just a king but was seen as a magician with supernatural powers and these powers were tied to traditional religions. These traditional religions were tied to places at this time which made it hard to take religion with you when you resettled or traveled and made it difficult to put this religion somewhere else. Furthermore, the idea of religion and honor are closely linked which points to something higher than us, to supernatural powers. 
	The conquering of Timbuktu brought new forms of culture and religion, more significantly Islam. Islam was delocalized and abstract which made it able to adapt to different ideas. Sunni Ali realized Islam’s potential as a unifying agent for the Songhay empire and its ability to be incorporated into local customs and religious ideas, blending with local saints and local holy places to form new rituals. This incorporation applied to an elite group of people and not to the masses. However, these localizations are still visible in the area today similar to what we saw in the Indian Ocean region in an earlier course. The process of localization was long and complex. More importantly, the result is not a watered down version of Islam but rather its own unique thing.  
	The speaker referred to a northern area of Songhay known as Bidan and a southern area known as Sudan. These areas mixed together with both benefits and negative consequences. The combination had the potential to make Songhay very strong or tear it apart. Eventually, the warrior estate turned on the Muslim estate because Muslim scholars felt threatened when their ideas on what it meant to be Muslim were challenged. In 1591, Morocco sent soldiers south to Songhay and the Songhay Empire, which was then wrecked by its own civil war, had to then fight the more modern Moroccan army. Morocco was successful and the Songhay Empire collapsed.
