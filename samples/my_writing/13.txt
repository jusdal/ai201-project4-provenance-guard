DIFFERING PERSPECTIVES

As referenced above, Edgerton’s findings must be placed in the context of the longstanding debate within the study of mental illness and psychopathology across cultures. This debate hinges upon two opposing perspectives: universalism and relativism.  

2.1: UNIVERSALISM

Universalism is closely aligned with the psychiatric realm of mental health research and its basic claim is that the same kind of psychopathology can be found across cultures because humans have a shared universal human nature and biology. Psychiatric disorders and syndromes have core symptoms that come together and fall into universal syndromal patterns (Canino & Alegría, 2008: 237). Although the symptoms may be slightly different in different cultures, the underlying psychopathology is the same across cultures. Thus, any differences in symptomatology observed across cultures are deemed superficial. Townsend (1982) argues that this approach “tends to view mental disorders as diseases and the deviant behaviors characteristic of mental disorders are symptoms of these diseases” which locate the causes of deviance within individuals mirroring the biomedical model of health (786). Dominant psychopathological classification systems used in diagnosing mental illness such as The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders and The International Classification of Diseases strengthened this perspective and led to a globalized psychiatric discourse. Essentially, the universalist discourse advocates taking these Western-derived professional classifications, or nosologies, as a starting point and checking their fit among varying other cultures. In this way, the universalist perspective can be viewed as an etic approach to mental illness. By examining the notions of madness in Africa and then comparing and equating them to Western conceptions of madness/schizophrenia, Edgerton strengthened this universalist perspective. Many subsequent researchers, especially psychiatrists, have agreed with the universalist interpretation of Edgerton’s research, largely rejecting the relativist view that culture could play any large role in conceptions of mental illness. 

Odejide (1979) directly references Edgerton in agreement and argues that it is “the mode of describing symptoms in psychiatric disorders” that is culturally and environmentally influenced and not the disorder itself. The symptom of delusions in Western cultures, for example, may be related to electronic bugging while delusions in tribal Nigeria may be related to supernatural powers but they are essentially the same symptom (104). Based on the similarity of symptoms across different cultures and the stable prevalence rate of schizophrenia across time and space, he concludes that “there is little evidence in support of the theory of cultural relativity...in the diagnosis of psychiatric disorders (107).

Murphy (1994),  based on her research in the Arctic and West Africa, came to a similar conclusion suggesting that “the phenomenal processes of disturbed thought and behavior similar to schizophrenia are found throughout most cultures…[and] are sufficiently distinctive and noticeable that almost everywhere a name has been created for them” (50). 
Referencing the International Pilot Study of Schizophrenia from 1975 and its main claim that the “core syndrome of symptoms identified as schizophrenia was found to exist everywhere,”
she asserts that her and Edgerton’s additional qualitative research gives the claim more significance and validity (50). 

Cohen et al. (2016) also confirm Edgerton’s findings explicitly saying “our findings are consistent with research findings that go back to Edgerton’s work in the 1960s” (11). In their survey of cases of psychosis in three sites (India, Nigeria, and Trinidad), they conclude that their findings “suggest that concepts of madness have similar features across diverse settings” although there were slight variations across the sites (11). 

2.2: RELATIVISM

Contrary to the universalist approach, the relativist approach is emic in nature and starts with local definitions and uses ethnographic methods to unearth local conceptualizations while simultaneously acknowledging the possible fluid boundaries of mental illness. While universalists primarily use non-Western cultures as simply a testing ground for Western theories, relativists aim to understand psychopathology within the cultural framework in which it manifests itself (Winders, 2013: 3). Given that universalism is the dominant perspective because of the introduction and spread of psychiatry in the 18th- and 19th-centuries across the world, relativism was largely a critique of universalism and of transcultural psychiatry which includes Edgerton’s study. Several researchers have taken issue with its dominance in framing mental illness.
