LOCALIZING ISLAM IN MAYOTTE

Islamic Prayer Across the Indian Ocean: Inside & Outside the Mosque is a book that seeks to answer this question. It examines, with a focus on the performative effects of prayers, practical variations of what falls under the label of Islam. In the introduction, Parkin (2000) lays out a number of dualisms present in Islamic discourses–such as pious/impious, allowed/disallowed practices, formal/mystic Islam–that hinge upon one prominent divergence: prayers inside the mosque vs. those outside the mosque. Prayers inside the mosque represent the global forms of Islam and are characterized by (somewhat-)standardized bodily practices while prayers outside the mosque are characterized by different forms of prayer and local enactments of these prayers while still retaining the global (Arabic) name for the prayer. Parkin also highlights that prayer is an embodied experience. Salat, for example, is one form of prayer that aligns with prayer inside the mosque because it relies on specific bodily movements that are observable and can serve to distinguish correct from improper Muslims and Muslims from non-Muslims.

Lambek (2000) picks up this notion of the performance of prayer with his specific ethnographic example of the way in which Islam has been localized on the island of Mayotte. While most historians view the localization of Islam in this region as a conflictual relationship between local social custom and Islamic law, he frames the context of Mayotte first by outlining three forms of knowledge present there–(1) Sufi Islamic traditions, (2) cosmology & magical powers, and (3) spirit possession–and looking at their interactions (51). Furthermore, he clarifies his chosen terminology–that people of Mayotte did not convert to Islam but rather accepted Islam–by explaining that the process was collective rather than individualized and that there was not a defined religion that they converted from. 

His main argument is that “the performance of prayer of including various forms of recitation has been the critical means by which Islam has been localised, rendered central to by the conduct and understanding of social relations in Mayotte, and conversely the critical means by which social relations have been Islamicised” (63). Lambek points to many examples of Islamic ritual prayers that are deeply embedded within the social organization of Mayotte. In other words, these performances of prayer are reinforcing and are incorporated into the social fabric of Mayotte according to existing scenarios of kinship and community (84). Drawing on Tambiah’s (1985) work, he explains that the recitation aspect of the prayer performance provides the primary means though which Islam was localized as evidenced above. Another element, the illocutionary aspect of the performance, is how the social order becomes Islamicized (71). These performative actions create social realities. This becomes apparent in the Maulida example in which balanced reciprocity is paramount and the Mayotte society becomes more egalitarian.  The line dividing religion from kinship, friendship, and citizenship becomes indistinguishable.

One final point that Lambek makes is the tension between Islam and spirit possession in the annual Maulida ritual for the Sakalava monarch Andriantsoly who converted to Islam. Ndramanavaka is the spirit of this monarch, known as a tromba or ancestral spirit, and enters into a Mayotte person. Other tromba spirits join as do lulu ny Maulida, another kind of spirit  who are Comorian Mulsim rulers. Spirit possession is deeply at odds with Islam and, in this ritual, “the balance between Islam and Sakalava spirit possession remains a delicate one, that it is, quite literally, a dance of power” (Tehindrazanarivelo, 1997 in Lambek: 93). Lambek notes that the message provided by the performance is not clear and that it is not really clear which system has incorporated or converted the other, contrary to the examples above.
