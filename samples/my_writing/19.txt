INTRODUCTION

I have been thinking about which sociocultural phenomena in my city I would like to focus on for quite some time. After going down a few paths that did not feel right for this project, I have finally decided to take a look at something that is personally meaningful and pragmatic, yet something that I believe will lead to interesting anthropological insights. Since moving here, I have found it somewhat difficult to make friends and even more difficult to find people to date. A large number of people I have talked to about this express similar concerns. I am very curious about the ways in which gay and bisexual men living in the city find potential partners. I became even more intrigued when I heard from a German friend recently that the city’s only LGBTQ+ bar will be closing soon. 

I began to think about the reasons it was closing down and, more importantly, what that would mean for gay and bisexual men living in the city. I started asking questions and doing some preliminary research. I asked friends about the bar and they all said that they have rarely, if ever, been to this bar and they seemed to look down on it with disapproval. I finally visited the bar and there were only four people there on a Friday night. If the LGBT+ people of the city are not here in this physical space, where are they? And how do you go about finding them? I started to think more broadly about contemporary dating practices and began to question if there is a need for physical gay spaces anymore particularly in the context of finding dating partners in the city. All of the people I have talked to so far–including gay/bisexual men and heterosexual women–have indicated that they use technology in some way to find potential dating partners. 

It is true that smartphones have drastically transformed nearly every aspect of life including dating. While once restricted to sitting in front of a computer to find a partner with internet dating, smartphone dating applications, such as Tinder, have mobilized the dating process, creating digital spaces layered on top of geographical ones (Blackwell and Birnholtz 2015; Duguay 2017). Although smartphone dating apps vary, geosocial networking apps, by definition, use location services to determine the approximate distance of users from one another. Grindr, which is the most popular app among gay and bisexual men in the world, displays individuals that are nearby on a screen with the closest individuals appearing first in the list. Depending on the density of your current location, users could be as close as a few meters and as far away as a few kilometers.
