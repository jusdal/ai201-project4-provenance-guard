INTRODUCTION

Even with a new transcultural, inter-disciplinary shift in psychiatric research that led to new data about previously unknown regions in the 1960s, anthropologist Robert B. Edgerton (1966) noted the marked absence of Africa within this research.  Although there were a few introductory studies, there was no comprehensive literature review and, more significantly for Edgerton, virtually no research on how Africans themselves “perceive[d], define[d] and respond[ed] to mental disorder in their own perceptual-conceptual terms” (409). Without this specific localized knowledge, Edgerton argued that it was impossible to know whether the “nosologies of Western psychiatry may distort African social and cultural reality” (409). 

To begin to fill in this gap, Edgerton surveyed over 500 people from four East African tribes: the Sebei of southwest Uganda, the Pokot of northwest Kenya, the Kamba of south central Kenya, and the Hehe of southwest Tanganyika. His data led to some interesting findings. Perhaps the most important is that, although there were some tribal differences, the behaviors that are representative of psychosis were notably similar in all 4 tribes and these behaviors were not so different from Western symptomatology, especially schizophrenia (420). While the causes and treatments for mental illness differed significantly from those of the West, he was surprised by how similar the symptoms detailed by the informants were to Western symptoms of psychosis. Reflecting on the results, he candidly notes his surprise stating:
	It is remarkable how alike these African conceptions of psychosis are to the Western 
European psychoses, particularly to the constellation of reactions known as schizophrenia. The Africans of these 4 tribes do not regard a single behavior as psychotic which could not be so regarded in the West. That is, they do not produce symptoms which are understandable as psychotic only within the context of their own cultures. What is psychotic for them would be psychotic for us. (415)

Despite his acknowledgement that his results could support either a universalist or relativist perspective on mental illness, his findings leaned heavily toward a universalist perspective and many subsequent researchers have referenced his work to outline and support their theories of universality in mental illness. This paper will examine to what extent this universalist perspective on psychopathology is still shared by contemporary researchers some 60 years later and will shed light on how Edgerton’s call for more data has been answered but has still not led to a definitive conclusion in the universalist-relativist debate within the discipline of psychopathology and current research has focused on moving past this binary to a combined approach.
