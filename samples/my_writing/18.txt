A Summary of Pierre Bourdieu’s “The Forms of Capital”

In his 1989 essay “The Forms of Capital,” Pierre Bourdieu explains his theory of capital, going beyond just the economic component of capital–money, assets, property–that was the focus of so many disciplines including sociology. He is critical of theories relying only on economic capital and argues that, while economic capital is important, “it is in fact impossible to account for the structure and functioning of the social world unless one reintroduces capital in all its forms and not solely in the one form recognized by economic theory” (241). Therefore, other noneconomic forms of capital must be defined and included to accurately describe and analyze a society and its structure. Bordeiu explicates two distinct categories of noneconomic capital: cultural capital and social capital. 
Cultural capital, although not defined explicitly by Bordeiu, can be inferred to mean capital related to a person’s knowledge, education, and skills. Cultural capital can exist in three different forms: (1) the embodied state, (2) the objectified state, and (3) the institutionalized state. Cultural capital in the embodied state refers to that which a person knows and can do and is linked to the body. It usually takes a long time to acquire and cannot be gained secondhand or indirectly. It also cannot be transmitted instantaneously as opposed to economic capital which can be exchanged immediately. Cultural capital in the objectified state refers to a person’s property or cultural goods (pictures, books, dictionaries, instruments, machines, etc.). Contrary to embodied cultural capital, objectified cultural capital can be transferred instantaneously and can be turned into economic capital quite easily but these objects also have a symbolic component that makes them cultural capital. A painting can be easily be sold for money, for example, but they buyer must be educated about the significance of the painting to effectively transfer the cultural capital of the painting. Cultural capital in the institutionalized state refers to the objectification of a person’s cultural capital in the form of academic or professional qualifications. Because this type of cultural capital “confers on its holder a conventional, constant, legally guaranteed value with respect to culture,” it is easily converted to economic capital (248). 
Social capital is defined as “the aggregate of the actual or potential resources which are linked to possession of a durable network of more or less institutionalized relationships of mutual acquaintance and recognition” (248). In other words, social capital is who you know and can encompass family, class, school, political party, social clubs, etc. One’s social capital is determined by the size of his social network, the sum of the network’s resources, and how quickly those resources can be accessed. Social capital takes a long time to cultivate, similar to embodied cultural capital, and must constantly be maintained in order to be used effectively in the future. 
Finally, Bordeiu examines how all of these forms of capital can be acquired, exchanged, and converted. He concludes that economic capital is at the root of all the other types of capital and cultural and social capital can be derived from economic capital through varying degrees of transformation involving time, attention, care, concern, etc. Bordieu explains that economic capital is concealed when it is converted to social and cultural capital and its profit is not expressed in the short-term but only in the long-term. Finally, Bordieu applies the principle of the conservation of energy to capital and states that “profits in one area are necessarily paid for by costs in another,” indicating the complex but interdependent nature of the different forms of capital.
