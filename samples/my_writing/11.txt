Throughout the past century, media has become quite pervasive.  As more and more people tune in to television and media content, the more important media effects research becomes.  As such, there has been an abundance of research done on the effects of media and many important conclusions have been drawn.  One area of research has not been so clear, however: stereotyping.  Stereotyping is a complex topic that must be looked at carefully, analyzing it from various angles and perspectives.  The issue of stereotyping will be explored in this paper, focusing specifically on the portrayal of one character from the recent Oscar-winning film Crash. 
              Crash garnered much critical acclaim after its release because of its controversial plot.  Set in the melting plot of Los Angeles, Crash explores race relations and cultural interference in the city.  The film takes a heart-wrenching look at everyday interactions as several subplots occur simultaneously over the course of two days, each eventually weaving into the larger plot of the film.  While some critics argue that the film perpetuates stereotypes, the filmmakers’ intent was undoubtedly to provide a dialogue about race and to shatter some commonly-held stereotypes.  Whether successful or not in diminishing stereotyping, the film did spark interest in the issue and allowed a forum for the discussion of race in America and Los Angeles.
              Despite sparking a conversation, the story increased stereotyping in certain instances as some groups in the film were narrowly depicted and were shown as rather one-dimensional.  Instead of painting a complex portrait of a specific group, certain attributes of that group were highlighted and exaggerated.  Jean Cabot, played by Sandra Bullock, was one character that suffered from an unbalanced depiction.  Jean represented a typical middle-class white woman and, more broadly, the white race.   As such, her representation of the white race will be analyzed, focusing on her role of increasing the stereotyping of white people. 
              Before delving into the analysis, however, it is imperative to establish definitions for certain terms, most importantly the term stereotype.  As discussed previously, researchers have not been in absolute agreement when discussing stereotyping and its existence in media.  Their disagreement is deeply rooted as they debate even the very definition of stereotype.  Consequently, there remains no ubiquitous definition that appeases all researchers.  Thus, for the purposes of this paper, I will define stereotype in the same manner as Harding et al. (1969).  They define a stereotype as “a belief that is simple, inadequately grounded, or at least partially inaccurate, and held with considerable assurance by many people” (as cited in Smith, 2009).  No regard is given to whether that belief is positive or negative or to which group holds the belief.  Therefore, the stereotype can be favorable or unfavorable and can be held by a minority group or a majority group.
              Expanding on this definition, I would like to add the component of negativity--positive depictions vs. negative depictions.  A negative depiction would be defined as a character that displays stereotypes--positive or negative--that have the potential to create negative media effects.  Further, a negative depiction focuses on a few unattractive qualities in a character while neglecting his or her other qualities.  This provides an unbalanced, and therefore negative, depiction of the character.  Based on this definition, I would argue that Jean Cabot is negatively depicted and that, through this negative depiction, she perpetuates stereotypes.  Because her character’s negative attributes are exaggerated and could cause negative media effects, she is considered a negative depiction.
              In Jean’s first scene, for example, one major stereotype perpetuated by her character is made clear: paranoia and unwarranted fear towards ethnic minorities.  When walking in Westwood with her husband, she moves closer to him and clutches her purse as she passes two young African-American men. This action is emphasized further by the dialogue of one of the African-American men. He says, “Did you see what that woman just did?...This white woman sees two black guys who look like UCLA students strolling down the sidewalk and her reaction is blind fear” (Haggis, 2004).  Thus, Jean’s attribute of fear, specifically fear of African-American men, is highlighted.  Her paranoia and unwarranted fear are focused on throughout the remainder of the film as, at another point, she lashes out at her husband for having the door locks changed by a young Hispanic man.  She fears that the man will take a copy of the key and sell it to his friends so that they can come and rob them.  Although there are slight attempts to uncover other attributes of her character, they are unsuccessful and are overshadowed by her fear of ethnic minorities.
