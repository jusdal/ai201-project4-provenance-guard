RESEARCH QUESTION

At the broadest level, I would like to know if intimacy and meaningful relationships can be cultivated between gay and bisexual men in the city through these geosocial networking apps and how this process differs from searching for a partner and building intimacy in a physical space. I have developed three research questions based on this inquiry:

How do gay/bisexual men in the city perceive geosocial digital applications as an alternative to physical spaces?
What are the motivations for using geosocial digital applications in the city and are these experiences fulfilled?
Do gay/bisexual men in the city find geosocial digital applications a viable tool for building meaningful relationships?

THEORETICAL REFLECTIONS

3.1: CONSTRUCTING THE FIELD

One important aspect for designing an anthropological research project is the determination of ‘the field.’ The concept of fieldwork emerged as the defining feature of anthropology from its inception as a discipline and the idea of ‘the field’ in ethnographic fieldwork has remained largely the same since the introduction of the concept by Malinowski. Gupta & Ferguson (1997, p.1) note the lasting importance of ‘the field’ in research today by stating the “single most significant factor determining whether a piece of research will be accepted as (that magical word) ‘anthropological’ is the extent to which it depends on experience ‘in the field.’” ‘The field’ in this case is centered around the idea of a geographically distinct and distant research site–a place away–that facilitates long-term face-to-face contact with exotic people deemed as others. 

Modern anthropologists, however, have challenged this strict interpretation of a spatialized, exotic field and noted the problems it poses to contemporary anthropological research. 
Caputo (2000), for example, examines how this rigid definition of ‘the field’ determines the type of research that can be completed and limits the anthropological knowledge that can be produced establishing a kind of hierarchy where ethnographic fieldwork is ranked based on authenticity and value, assessed by how far away the field is and how exotic its people. Research conducted in remote, unfamiliar villages, for example, are viewed as more authentic and more valuable than research conducted in urban environments that are more familiar. Research done away is more valuable than that done at home. Gupta & Ferguson (1997) also criticize this view of ‘the field’ by deconstructing this spatialized notion and the field and  pointing out that our world is becoming more interconnected and geographically distinct places no longer limit culture within its borders making it difficult to put a geographical boundary around your research.

These criticisms of how ‘the field’ is defined are especially relevant to my project. Similar to Caputo, my research will be done at home and this could affect its perceived authenticity and value to the field of anthropology. In contrast to Gupta & Ferguson’s (1997) critique, the idea of a geographical space as a boundary of the field is upheld in my case because I have decided to bound my research to that of gay and bisexual men living in the city. This does not mean, however, that the culture within this boundary or field is homogenized or grouped as the city’s population is quite diverse because of the university population. This is precisely the point that Gupta & Ferguson are making: specific cultures cannot be tied to a particular location anymore. In addition, the fact that my research observes relationships taking place in a digital space which corresponds to geographical space, however, complicates the idea of marking my field along geographical barriers. I will thus use my research questions to firmly establish the boundaries of the field by focusing on specific geosocial networking platforms that are the most popular in the city. Preliminary research within my group of friends suggested Grindr, PlanetRomeo, and Tinder are the most used in the city.
