Self-Study: A Review of Lectures 1-7

As a social institution, marriage is defined by a social group’s norms, rules, and practices and is legitimated through their history, language, customs, and laws. Therefore, marriage is difficult to define universally as the concept of marriage can change greatly across the world and in different cultures. As history demonstrates, the institution of marriage can be transformed or altered by redefining these norms and rules and by changing the practices and behaviors of marriage. Marriage has been analyzed through several different anthropological perspectives throughout history. In the 19th century, for instance, evolutionist anthropologists examined marriage in the context of civilized versus primitive societies. In the 1950s, the structural-functionalist perspective emerged and marriage was examined through theoretical models based on the social function of marriage in a society. Around this same time, the structuralist perspective was developed by Claude Lévi-Strauss and Louis Dumont and theoretical models were constructed based on the idea that marriage is an expression of the mental structures and values of the social group. Shortly after this, a post-structuralist perspective was introduced when Pierre Bourdieu examined marriage not just by its function or structure but also by the way in which it is practiced strategically in everyday life through their actions.
 As these different perspectives were introduced, different categories of kinship and marriage were constructed and defined. Endogamy and exogamy are two such categories of marriage and refer specifically to the rules applied to marriage. In endogamy, one is expected to marry within one’s own social group while, in exogamy, one is expected to marry outside one’s social group. There can be many forms of endogamy/exogamy as the term social group is a very broad term and can be defined in many different ways (i.e. caste/sub-caste, class, family, tribe, race, religion). When looked at through Dumont’s structuralist perspective, for example, caste (and sub-caste) endogamy/exogamy becomes the set of rules for marriage as his theory of caste and purity/impurity is central to his understanding of social life in India. These rules of marriage are important because they help identify values and structure in a society. Endogamy is advantageous because it maintains the homogeneity and resources of the group. Exogamy is advantageous because it expands the group’s social network and gives access to additional resources through the alliance of marriage. One example of these rules in practice is in the Paraiyar caste of Tamil Nadu, India. As illustrated by Robert Deliége in “Caste without a System: A Study of South Indian Harijans,” the Paraiyars mostly practice caste and religious endogamy by marrying not only within the Paraiyar caste but also within their religious group (Hindu / Catholic) through patrilineal cross cousin marriages.
