GLOBALIZATION

Historically, anthropologists have studied places bounded by geography. If one wanted to study India, for example, he would go to India and look at ‘Indian’ culture. As such, culture had primarily been conceptualized as being attached to a specific, fixed geographic location and was rather homogenous. With a rise in technology and the migration of peoples from one place to another, the idea of globalization emerged  in the 1990s. Anthropologist Arjun Appadurai (1996) lays out his theory of globalization in his chapter titled ‘Disjuncture and Difference in the Global Cultural Economy.’ He begins by briefly examining globalization in a historical context noting that, as noted above, culture was once restricted by geography, ecology, and even active resistance against the Other as in Japan (47). Warfare and religion led to much of the cultural exchange from the past while advancements in technology have increased cultural exchange exponentially in today’s world. Appadurai asserts that prevailing Eurocentric modernization theories like Americanization and the convergence of culture into one global homogenous culture (see Taylor, 1995) are too simple to capture the current global flows of culture and are no longer valid . “The United States,” he argues, “is no longer the puppeteer of a world system of images but is only one node of a complex transnational construction of imaginary landscapes” held together by parallel circulations of mass-mediated images, scripts, and sensations (50). To deal with this new complexity, he argues that global cultural flows should be looked at according to five fluid, overlapping dimensions: ethnoscapes, mediascapes, technospaces, financescapes, ideoscapes.

Although historians were at first skeptical of the globalization debate in anthropology, historian Brigitte Reinwald (2002), like Risso discussed above, picked up on Appadurai’s concept of scapes and applied it to the spreading of ideas and culture in the Indian Ocean region or, in her words, the “cognitive and material practices that generated non-territorial ‘spaces’ and ‘translocal discourse communities’ or merchants, workers, and religious scholars” in this region (10). Like Appadurai, she uses the idea of a landscape when looking at the Indian Ocean region because it allows a more fluid look at a space relating it to “both a material and an imaginative practice, or experience of places” (12). The idea of a landscape is useful because it captures the flow and exchange rather than a less complex model that would oversimplify cultural exchange by excluding people that “generate conflicting...cultural narratives” (Glassman, 1995: 17 qtd. in Reinwald). Focusing on agency, Reinwald’s coins her notion of the Indian Ocean region as a seascape that is simultaneously local and global and “accounts for different--converging and conflicting--ways of relating to and representing this historical, social and cultural space” (18). Both Appadurai and Reinwald advocate for a more complex view of spaces and cultural exchange with a focus on bilateral flows rather than just a unilateral flow. This was a novel idea in the domain of history as Reinwald questioned if globalization was actually a new phenomena, as Appadurai suggested, or whether it could be applied to a historical context, namely the Indian Ocean region. 

According to Risso (1995), Islam was especially well suited to seafaring merchants who needed to conduct complex transactions and to travel because it was portable and not tied to a certain geographic location. On the spread of Islam through this region she states:
 Islam not only sustained minority Muslim merchant communities in non-Muslim regions but also attracted many merchant converts. It can be argued that Islam made possible a commercial hegemony in the Indian ocean region. Muslim networks became so successful that they pushed aside older patterns of trade. Those merchants who were shut out or marginalized by the networks were more likely to convert: success bred success. (6)
Thus, Islam became a defining characteristic of this seascape and can be viewed through the lense of globalization theory. Was the Islamification of this region, however, one of conversion to a homogenous religion or was it more nuanced as Appadurai and Reinwald have suggested in their globalization critiques?
