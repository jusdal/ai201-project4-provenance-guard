INTRODUCTION
 
This paper will first lay out how the audience has been framed in media studies and then examine how this framing affects studies dealing with conceptions of national identity through television.


FRAMING OF THE AUDIENCE IN MEDIA STUDIES

3.1: MASS MEDIA AND THE HOMOGENOUS AUDIENCE

To get a sense of how the audience is conceptualized and how contemporary media anthropologists frame the audience, we must first go back to the beginning of media studies when mass media was just beginning to be looked at critically. Theodor Adorno and Max Horkheimer (1947) of the influential Frankfurt School were some of the first to theorize about mass media and its audience. Having experienced the Nazi regime’s rise to power, Adorno & Horkheimer were especially interested in understanding how ideology was spread and how reality was obscured and were deeply influenced by the Marxist perspective. Moving to the United States, they turned their critical gaze to capitalism and the Hollywood film industry.They defined their concept of "culture industry" in the fourth chapter of their book The Dialectic of Enlightenment which describes how popular culture in a capitalist society functions similar to an industry by producing products which are standardized. They are very critical of the modern capitalist system and examine the way in which mass media serves as a way of controlling people and maintaining the status quo of the capitalist society. Mass media is commodified and standardized specifically in form/style, and every film, song, tv show, etc. is essentially the same, despite the seemingly different content. True art inspires critical thinking and challenges the status quo and Adorno and Horkheimer's argument is that the things being produced no longer do that. Thus, they make a distinction between true art and mass media. Their argument corresponds to Gramsci's notion of hegemony and the notions of convergence theories of culture in modernization.
